# Historical Design Brief

These notes guide future agent proposals and ending revisions. They are design constraints for historical plausibility, not final scenario text.

## Shiloh Is a Priority Gap

Shiloh should become a major Western Theater stage. It is one of the most important under-discussed battles of the war for this game because it shows the strategic problem that Eastern victories could not solve.

Design points for agents:

- The battle should not be treated as a side episode. It is a turning point in the West.
- Albert Sidney Johnston's death matters because the Confederacy lost a commander who might have been able to shape the Army of Tennessee into a more cohesive force.
- The stage should highlight the contrast between Lee's later Army of Northern Virginia and the fractured Western command system.
- The loss of Western Tennessee, river control, rail access, depots, and regional industry should matter in campaign pressure.
- The stage should teach that Confederate success in Virginia could coexist with strategic collapse in the West.
- Shiloh should feed later Western Theater scenarios: Chickamauga, Chattanooga, Atlanta/election pressure, and the late-war supply crisis.

Suggested scenario ID:

```text
shiloh_army_of_tennessee
```

Core question:

```text
Can the Confederacy turn the surprise at Shiloh into a decisive Western victory while preserving command cohesion and strategic follow-through?
```

## Ending Realism

The ending model should distinguish three very different outcomes:

1. Full Confederate independence
2. Negotiated or status quo peace followed by reunion
3. Defeat, parole, exhaustion, or fragmented resistance

Full Confederate independence should be rare. It likely requires both:

- A decisive defeat of Union armies in the field, preferably on Northern soil or in a way that makes continued prosecution politically untenable.
- British and French diplomatic entry, recognition, mediation, or coercive pressure after a major Confederate victory.

The most plausible alternate routes are:

- A Peace Democrat win in 1864.
- British and French intervention after a major Confederate battlefield success.
- McClellan or another peace-aligned political outcome producing armistice pressure.
- A negotiated settlement that ends the war without immediate independence, including possible reunion under face-saving or status quo terms.

Agents should not imply that marginally better battlefield performance automatically creates independence. The South doing better in the field might plausibly produce a negotiated settlement, a temporary armistice, parole arrangements, or reunion under altered political conditions before it produces recognized sovereignty.

## Monroe Doctrine / Texas Peace Concept

Agents may use the historical idea of a status quo peace or diversionary settlement as inspiration: Northern peace proposals and press speculation sometimes imagined ending the war through a redirection of both armies toward a foreign-policy project such as enforcing the Monroe Doctrine in Mexico/Texas. In game terms, this should be treated as a political peace concept, not as a clean military victory.

Possible mechanics or endings:

- `Status Quo Peace`
- `Peace Democrat Armistice`
- `Foreign Mediation`
- `Monroe Doctrine Diversion`
- `Reunion with Conditions`
- `Recognized Independence`

Only `Recognized Independence` should score as full Confederate strategic victory, and it should require a very high bar.

## Fredericksburg Winter Politics

Agents proposing late-1862 or early-1863 content should remember that Fredericksburg was not only a battlefield victory. It was also a winter political and administrative hinge.

Design points:

- The battle should feed directly into winter camps, furlough politics, supply discipline, and state-versus-Richmond management friction.
- The famous Rappahannock fraternization, burial flags, newspaper exchanges, and Christmas-week contact should be treated as humane pauses inside an ongoing war, not as sentimental proof that the conflict was softening.
- A good Fredericksburg stage should force tradeoffs between immediate offensive follow-up and preserving the army for the 1863 campaign season.

## Jackson Survives / Gettysburg What-If

Agents working on Gettysburg should use the public's strongest intuitive counterfactual correctly.

Design points:

- Jackson surviving Chancellorsville should change operational tempo, command cohesion, and the handling of high ground and road nets before it changes abstract morale numbers.
- The what-if is strongest when it affects Cemetery Hill, Culp's Hill, the Baltimore Pike, and the timing of concentration, not when it becomes generic magic-victory writing.
- Jackson surviving may also justify a wider Pennsylvania pressure campaign rather than a fixed Cemetery Ridge climax.

## Atlanta / Election Pressure

Agents proposing mid-to-late 1864 Western Theater content should treat Atlanta as both an operational and political hinge.

Design points:

- The campaign should emphasize that Johnston's retreats were politically infuriating but strategically intelligible. Trading space for time was one of the few ways to keep Sherman from turning Atlanta into a clean Northern political triumph before the election.
- Replacing Johnston with Hood should feel like a real strategic temptation: more offensive spirit, more political clarity, and a materially greater risk of wrecking the Army of Tennessee.
- Atlanta is not just a city objective. It is a rail, depot, workshop, and election objective at the same time.
- If a proposal treats Atlanta only as Sherman's tactical puzzle and ignores Lincoln's 1864 political vulnerability before the city's fall, it is incomplete.
- Agents may use Cold Harbor, Spotsylvania, and Atlanta together as the core trio for explaining why 1864 became a war of attrition, maneuver, and elections rather than one single decisive field battle.
